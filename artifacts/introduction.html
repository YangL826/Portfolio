Self-Introduction: The Same Story, In My Own Voice

During a three-day onsite intensive, a cohort peer interviewed me and introduced me to the class in his own words. The assignment that followed was to take what he heard and surfaced — and rewrite it as my own introduction, in the first person, for my own portfolio.

Introduction

Hearing your own story from someone else first.

My first artifact shows I can carry a technical problem to a deployed AI product. This one exercises a different, easier-to-overlook skill: owning and adapting my own professional narrative.

As part of our onsite intensive at Indiana Wesleyan University, cohort members interviewed one another and introduced their partner to the class. A peer interviewed me and, from a short conversation, introduced me in the third person. Before I ever wrote my own bio, I heard someone else's compressed version of my career first. The exercise afterward was to take the same underlying material — the same facts, the same throughline — and reclaim it in my own voice, written for the audience of this portfolio rather than spoken to a room.

Description

My introduction, in my own words

I came to the United States from China after finishing my PhD in Earth Sciences at UCLA, where my research asked how AI could help answer questions the Earth sciences hadn't been able to answer on their own — and I'm proudest of the ones we actually solved, not the ones that just made a good demo. That same instinct carried me into healthcare, where I worked as a data scientist applying much the same approach I'd built for geophysical problems to clinical and biomarker data instead. I'm now in Indiana Wesleyan University's M.S. in Artificial Intelligence program, sharpening the same throughline: I'm a scientist and engineer who works where AI meets discovery, whatever the domain turns out to be. Outside of work, I hike, watch movies, play games, and talk with friends — the last of which I'd argue is the most underrated of the four.

Objective

Why I chose it

I selected this as my second artifact because it demonstrates a competency my first artifact does not: owning and re-authoring my own professional narrative, rather than building a system. My objectives were to:

  • Confirm which parts of my career narrative — Earth science PhD → AI applied to science → healthcare data science → AI/ML graduate study — actually came through when someone else compressed it, and keep those parts.
  • Rewrite that same material as a first-person introduction suited to a written portfolio, rather than a live, spoken introduction to a classroom.
  • Practice adapting one consistent professional story across different audiences and formats — a skill distinct from, and complementary to, building an AI product end to end.
Process

How I made it

Pairing The instructor paired each student with a partner for a reciprocal interview-and-introduce exercise, on the first of three onsite days.
Interview My partner asked open questions — background, current studies, work history, life outside of work — and introduced me to the cohort from what he took away.
Reflect I compared what he chose to keep — the PhD, the pivot from Earth science to healthcare AI, the throughline of applying AI to hard scientific problems, one personal detail — against what I would have emphasized myself.
Rewrite I rebuilt the introduction in the first person, keeping the material that landed and sharpening the parts a listener's version had flattened or left generic.
Fit to audience I adjusted tone and pacing for a written portfolio bio rather than a spoken classroom introduction — different medium, same underlying story.
Skills demonstrated

What this exercised

  • Narrative ownershipTaking an external, secondhand version of my story and reclaiming it in my own voice rather than leaving it as someone else's summary.
  • Audience adaptationConverting a live, spoken classroom introduction into a written introduction for a professional portfolio audience — same facts, different medium and pacing.
  • Reflective revisionUsing what a listener kept and dropped as a signal for what to sharpen, rather than guessing at what matters.
  • Personal brandingDeciding, deliberately, which details establish credibility and which make the story memorable rather than a résumé recitation.
Value proposition

Why it matters

Technical depth doesn't explain itself, and neither does a career that has moved across domains — Earth science, healthcare, now AI. Someone will eventually have to tell my story when I'm not in the room: a hiring manager summarizing my background to a panel, a teammate introducing me to a new project. This exercise showed me which parts of that story survive being told secondhand, and let me take deliberate ownership of the parts that need my own voice to land.

That is the same "translation" skill I name as a core pillar of my value proposition — here practiced on my own story instead of someone else's technical work.

Relevance

A test you can run on your own narrative.

Anyone building a professional portfolio can use the same exercise: have someone else interview you cold and introduce you, then rewrite what they said in your own voice.

  1. Notice what a listener keeps. What they repeat back is what actually landed — keep it.
  2. Notice what they drop or flatten. That's the part you need to sharpen yourself; it wasn't communicated clearly enough to survive.
  3. Rewrite for your actual audience. A spoken classroom introduction and a written portfolio bio are not the same artifact, even when the underlying facts are identical.
