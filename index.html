Professional bio

From molecular simulation to models people actually use.

I am a data and machine learning professional with a Ph.D. from UCLA, where my research used molecular simulation, high-performance computing, and machine learning to model how materials behave under extreme conditions. Building models that embedded domain physics directly into the learning problem taught me a principle I have carried into every project since: the best applied AI is not the most complex model, but the one that respects what is already known about the problem.

I brought that approach to Merck, where I spent nearly two years as a data scientist in drug discovery — building models to predict drug concentration profiles, benchmarking machine learning approaches that shrank a diagnostic biomarker panel from ten peptides to two, and designing LLM and retrieval-augmented pipelines that cut the manual curation of experimental reports by roughly 80%. The through-line was translation: taking work that lived in a notebook and turning it into something a scientist could act on.

I am now pursuing an M.S. in Artificial Intelligence at Indiana Wesleyan University, deepening my work in large language models, generative AI, and the responsible application of both. I am drawn to problems where AI has to survive contact with real users and real consequences — healthcare, scientific research, and applied ML systems where being plausible is not the same as being right.

Personal value proposition

I build AI systems that survive contact with reality.

01 Scientific evaluation discipline

My training is in fields where a wrong answer is wrong no matter how confidently it is stated. In generative AI, where models produce output that is plausible and useless in equal measure, that instinct is not academic — it is the difference between a demo and a product.

02 Full-distance ownership

Ambiguous problem → framing → model → deployment → revision. I have done every step, on proprietary industrial problems and on projects small enough to hand you the link.

03 Translation

I have spent my career explaining computational work to people who do not do computational work — chemists, clinicians, decision-makers. Technical work that cannot be explained does not get adopted.
